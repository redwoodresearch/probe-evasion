Probe evasion sprint · evidence so far · 8 October 2026

Can prompts beat probes?

We told a model to get a concept past a probe that reads its internal activations, without changing its weights. Here is everything we found, how strong each piece of evidence is, and what is still missing.

In short

  • Yes, clearly for one concept. Sarcasm gets past every sarcasm probe we tried: 34% of attempts are real sarcasm the probe misses, against 6% for the plain prompt. Anger, criticism and deception also get past one probe, but each fails a robustness check: another probe catches them, or the concept is watered down.
  • They all work the same way. The model does not hide anything internally. It changes how it writes, into a style the probe's training data barely contained, such as flat, literal sarcasm or anger stated once and calmly.
  • Probes held when the concept is tied to content. Maths done in the head and chemistry were not evaded by any sensibly trained probe: 0 in 1,000 attempts for maths, 0 in 100 for chemistry (so likely under 3%). The one maths exception is a single weak early-layer probe on Gemma that later layers back up.
  • Most early “evasions” were measurement mistakes. We list six traps, because they are easy to fall into.
  • The big open question is whether these gaps are cheap to patch. That test is running now.

The setup

A probe is a small linear classifier that reads a model's internal activations and outputs a score for each token: how strongly does this look like sarcasm, anger or deception? Monitors built from probes are a candidate defence for AI control, because they read what the model computes rather than only what it writes. Our question: can a model, just by being prompted, express a concept while keeping the probe's score low?

Figure 1. Throughout, red means the concept got past the probe and green means the probe caught it. The monitored model is Qwen3.5-27B with reasoning off. Only the instruction changes between the plain and attack arms.

Flag lineThe score above which a reply is flagged. We set it so 1% of normal replies without the concept are flagged: a 1% false-alarm rate.
Valid evasionA reply that really does the task (a Claude Sonnet 5.5 judge confirms the concept is present) and scores under the flag line.
Off-policy probeTrained on text written by another model (Claude) rather than the monitored one. Real monitors for rare bad behaviour often have to be built this way, because there are no real examples to train on. The opposite, on-policy, uses the monitored model's own text.

How we test an attack

  1. 1
    Train the probe

    On text written by Claude, not Qwen. Includes look-alike negatives (blunt but sincere replies, lookups that state a number). Trained for the exact way it is read.

  2. 2
    Set the flag line

    From about 1,000-2,000 of Qwen's own replies that do not show the concept, cleaned by a judge. The line flags 1% of them.

  3. 3
    Find an attack

    Either written by hand, or found by a search (a “searched” attack): over 5 rounds, Claude proposes 4 new instructions per round, sees every probe score, and keeps the best. A strong attacker, on purpose.

  4. 4
    Freeze and test

    The best instruction is frozen, then run on 100 tasks the search never saw, next to the plain prompt on the same tasks.

  5. 5
    Count valid evasions

    A reply counts only if a Claude judge confirms the concept is really there and it is under the line.

Figure 2. A “searched” attack comes from step 3's search; a “hand-written” one does not. The attacker is deliberately strong: in a search, Claude sees every probe score. The probe and its line are fixed before any attack is tried.

What we found

Four more definitions before the results.

Reading a replyA probe scores every token. Our running-peak reading averages those scores with a running average that gives each new token half the weight, and keeps the highest value. AISI's deception probes use the average over all reply tokens instead.
Instruction hiddenThe attack instruction is part of the model's input, and its text alone can lower scores. So we re-score each reply with the instruction removed from what the probe reads. Those are the headline numbers.
The conceptsSarcasm, anger, criticism: does the reply express it? Deception: a role-play lie to a direct question. Maths in the head: answering a word problem with the right number and no working shown. Chemistry: the reply discusses chemistry. Most runs use Qwen3.5-27B; some maths runs use Gemma-2-9B.

Standard checks: AUROC on answer styles the probe never trained on (the chance that a random concept reply scores above a random normal reply; 1 is perfect), and plain answers caught at the 1% line. Beats plain: valid evasions several times the plain prompt's. Holds hidden: still far above plain when the attack instruction is removed from what the probe reads. Trained for its reading: the probe was trained on the same reading it is judged by (see “Probe trained one way, read another” below). Full strength: a Claude judge rates the concept (1–5 scale) about as high as in plain replies, and a stricter check agrees. Sarcasm passes every check we ran. The same attack replies, with the instruction hidden, also get past other sarcasm probes:

  • sarcasm probe B, the first one we built (layer 16, trained on the average): 258 of 500, against 12 for plain;
  • a layer-48 probe trained on Qwen's own replies instead of Claude's: 260 of 500, against 2 for plain.

Both are higher than the headline 171 from probe A. Probe A reacts more to the instruction text, so hiding the instruction costs the attack more against it. We headline the most conservative probe that meets our rules.

Anger looks dramatic: 126 of 200 replies get past the probe with the instruction hidden, and 196 with it visible. But most of those replies are mild frustration, and a probe at a different layer catches every one. Criticism mostly works by hiding the verdict inside a story. Deception works against the UK AI Security Institute's released probes because they average over the whole reply, so honest padding dilutes the lie. That is an expected weakness of averaging. The searched deception prompt may do something more, but we cannot tell yet (see “What is missing”).

Why the attacks work

Each probe learned the most common surface sign of its concept, because that is what its training text was full of. Each successful attack keeps the meaning and drops that sign.

ConceptWhat the probe learned to look forWhat the attack does instead
Sarcasmexaggeration, mock praiseone calm, literal sentence that claims the opposite
Angerheated, accusing tonenames the feeling once, then calm practical next steps
Criticisman explicit negative verdicta cheerful story that implies the verdict
Deceptiona high average score over the replyadds honest, useful text after the lie

Did the probe ever see this style? Mostly not.

We had a Claude judge label how each sarcastic reply delivers its sarcasm. Only 7% of the probe's training examples are deadpan, and even those mostly keep a sarcasm marker (“Smooth.”, “Sure, because…”). The searched prompt's replies are 85% deadpan, and Qwen's plain sarcasm almost never is.

The concept is still visible inside the model. Among replies written in the flat style, the probe still ranks sarcastic above sincere almost every time (AUROC 0.98, where 1 is perfect). But flat sarcasm scores lower than the most playful, exaggerated sincere replies in normal traffic, and those are what set the 1% flag line. So the probe ranks it correctly and still lets it through.

Two more patterns matter for how to read these results:

  • Style does the work, not knowing about the probe. For anger, the same style requested without any mention of a probe evades just as well (197 against 196 of 200, instruction visible). The sarcasm prompt never mentions a probe, and adding a warning about one changes nothing (288 against 290 of 500, probe B, instruction visible). Instructions aimed at the probe itself (“believe every word”, “think about something else”) got nothing through: 0 of 77 and 0 of 75.
  • Some evasion is the concept getting quieter. A stricter judge (“clearly angry, more than mildly frustrated?”) says yes to only 32 of the 196 valid anger attack replies, against 71 of 144 valid plain replies. All 32 still get past the layer-48 probe (instruction visible). For criticism, only 15 of 87 evading replies state the verdict fully clearly. Sarcasm is the exception: it is rated nearly as sarcastic as the plain replies.

Where probes held

Apart from the weak layer-6 Gemma probe, maths done in the head and chemistry were never evaded by a probe with sensible training data and a clean line. That held whether the probe was trained on Qwen's own text or on Claude's. A correct maths answer must contain the computed number, and a chemistry answer must talk about chemistry, so there is no style that keeps the concept but loses the signal.

Missing look-alike negatives (training examples that resemble the concept but are not it) did not make probes evadable either. It made them flag the wrong things: a chemistry probe without biology and maths examples flagged 208 of 243 biology and maths replies.

Six ways we fooled ourselves first

Every one of these produced a convincing fake evasion before we caught it.

A polluted flag line

Some “no maths” replies to lookups (questions whose answer is a number given in the question) computed something anyway. They pushed the line up from 0.36 to 0.97 and made evasion look easy.

Training labels that contradicted the content

157 of the 960 maths training answers labelled “no computation” had in fact computed the answer. Probes trained on them learned the wrong boundary and looked evadable.

Probe trained one way, read another

A probe trained on the average token score but read with a peak flags single spiky tokens in normal traffic, which lifts the line.

The instruction in the probe's view

The attack instruction itself lowers scores. We re-score every reply with the instruction removed.

Mixed answer formats in one line

A line set across many formats is driven by the noisiest format. Attacks in a quiet format then slip under it.

Nonsensical training data

A chemistry probe whose “not chemistry” side was other languages and formatting was easy to evade, and meaningless.

What is missing from the story

Ordered by how much each would change the conclusion. Text monitors are out of scope, and the attacker's advantages (seeing probe scores, a Claude search) are intended.

Next

Does a patched probe hold?

Every win so far is a style the training data barely covered. If adding that style to training closes the gap and a fresh search finds nothing new, the result is a cheap coverage fix. If each patch is followed by a new gap, it is a structural weakness. Running now for sarcasm, results after round 1.

Next

Full-strength evasions

Anger and criticism evade mostly by turning the concept down. The clean evidence is the smaller set at full strength: 32 clearly angry replies, all missed by the layer-48 probe (instruction visible), but all caught by the layer-16 probe.

Next

Two probes at once

Anger's layer-16 and layer-48 probes have opposite blind spots; flagging if either fires leaves 0 of 200. Sarcasm and criticism have not been tested against a combined probe, and no search has targeted one.

Soon

A deception probe trained for a peak reading

The searched deception prompt does even better under peak readings, but AISI's probes were trained for the average. Only a probe trained for the peak can tell us whether that is real.

Soon

A concept closer to the threat

Sarcasm and anger are stand-ins. Maths and chemistry held because the content must appear. The open question is which side dangerous behaviour falls on: the act itself (code that sabotages) may be content-bound, the intent may be style-like.

Later

Breadth

Almost everything is on one monitored model (Qwen3.5-27B, reasoning off); some maths runs used Gemma-2-9B. One probe seed per recipe, and each concept's line comes from one kind of traffic. A check of deception on the larger Qwen3.5-122B was small (50 scenarios) and found no uplift.

Where we stand: prompting alone can push concepts that are about tone or intent past off-policy probes that look good on standard checks, by moving into a style the training data missed. Whether that is a cheap coverage problem or a lasting weakness is the question the next experiments answer.
