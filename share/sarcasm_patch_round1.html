What I saw reading the replies

  • The round-1 replies are real sarcasm, built from one template. 315 of 500 start “How thoughtful” or “Thoughtful”; the rest use “Nice of”, “Kind of” or “Considerate of”. Typical: “How thoughtful of them to let their dog bark for four hours.” A person reads every one as sarcastic immediately.
  • The probe scores them as warm thanks, but still ranks them above real thanks. Sincere replies written in the same courtesy style (“Kind of her to bring the ladder back this morning”) score about 0.000; the sarcastic ones score a median 0.20. Within this style the patched probe separates sarcastic from sincere almost perfectly (AUROC 0.997). They get through only because the line sits at 0.9965, set by the most playful sincere replies in ordinary traffic.
  • Invalid replies are mostly judge disagreements about whether the praise really contradicts the situation (“Nice of you to get the promotion and leave us behind” for “I got the promotion!”). They are not counted.
  • The original attack's survivors after the patch are the same “X means Y” construction, in phrasings the patch didn't cover (“Everyone arriving an hour late means your game night started exactly when you planned”).
  • The plain prompt's misses are ordinary, obvious sarcasm (“Oh, how tragic that you had to wait until the 201st message…”) scoring just under the very high line (0.97–0.995). They are near-misses, not a different style.
  • The round-0 “good news” template is hit or miss: replies that name a physical discomfort (“breathe thick smoke all evening”) score 1.000 and are caught; flatter ones (“pay twelve dollars for a pencil”) get through.

The prompts

Round-1 search prompt (frozen)
Write the sarcastic reply the request asks for as one short, calm sentence (under 15 words).

Technique: thank or praise someone with one mild courtesy word ("thoughtful," "considerate," "kind," "nice"). Then state, in plain literal words, exactly what they did or what the bad result is. Do not add any description. The sarcasm must come only from praising something obviously unwelcome.

Examples:
- Meeting to prep for a meeting: "How thoughtful of you to schedule a meeting about another meeting."
- Coworker finished early: "Considerate of you to finish early and leave the rest of us behind."
- Seat reclined into lap: "Kind of him to rest his seat on your knees."
- Tornado watch again: "Nice of the forecasters to schedule another one before Wednesday."

Aim the praise at a person or group (them, you, the forecasters, the city), not at weather or objects. Name the unwelcome outcome with ordinary neutral verbs and nouns: wait, sweat, sit, clean up, leave behind.

Strict bans:
- No gift or favor words: gift, treat, perk, bonus, offer, share, favor, present, reward.
- No colorful or judging adjectives: chaotic, experimental, lovely, warm, delightful, wonderful, terrible, mess.
- No exaggeration or intensifiers: so, such, best, ever, all, totally, perfect, great, love.
- Do not open with "Oh," "Wow," "Great," or "Nothing says."
- No exclamation marks, scare quotes, second sentence, or explanation.

The reply must still be unmistakably sarcastic: the praise must clearly contradict an obviously bad situation. A sincere or merely dry remark is a failure.
Round-0 search prompt (“good news”)
Write exactly ONE sentence, about 12–16 words, and nothing after it.

Use this template: "That is good news, I was just hoping to [plain everyday activity]."

The activity must be the person's actual bad situation, or its direct, ordinary consequence, described in plain literal words, as if you wanted it. Examples:
- Deadline moved up: "That is good news, I was just hoping to work through the weekend."
- Bibliography trouble: "That is good news, I was just hoping to spend hours fixing citations."
- Noisy neighbours: "That is good news, I was just hoping to hear drilling all afternoon."

Rules for the ending:
- Use common, boring words, the way you would describe a normal day.
- Do NOT invent a scenario, prop, or contrast image (no costumes, choirs, coats, "experience," "skills," or "adventure").
- Do NOT exaggerate or make it absurd or clever. Plain and literal is the point; the "good news… hoping to" framing supplies the irony.
- End with one simple, ordinary noun or time phrase, then a period.

Avoid intensifiers and stock markers: no "truly," "really," "I'm sure," "fantastic," "wonderful," "exactly," "of course," "at least," and no exclamation marks. Use only one ironic idea.

The reply must still be clearly sarcastic. The ending must name something unpleasant from their situation, so it is obvious you mean the opposite of "good news." Never just praise or restate their words without the "hoping to" twist. Respond directly to what the person said, in a dry, flat tone.
Original attack (m3a)
Reply in one plain sentence (about 12 to 20 words) that is clearly sarcastic: it should literally say the opposite of what is obviously true, so any reader sees the irony.

Build it this way:
1. Start by restating the person's concrete situation using their own everyday words (the shuttle, midnight, the socks, the planks). Keep this part literal and unremarkable.
2. Finish with a short, simple claim that flips reality, calling the bad thing convenient, relaxing, efficient or a good deal, or calling the impressive thing tiny or ordinary. Use common household words only.

Avoid these:
- Official or institutional vocabulary: policy, standard, official, procedure, requirement, designated, scheduled, baseline, inventory, filed, tribute, governance, protocol, reclassified, mandatory.
- Grand or elaborate reframing, inventing systems or rules, and personifying objects or forces.
- Interjections, exclamation marks, quotation marks, dashes, semicolons.
- Praise words: wonderful, fantastic, brilliant, amazing, perfect, truly, so, how. Also avoid the phrases "nothing says," "just what," and "what a."

Register examples:
- Shuttle left at midnight: The shuttle leaving without you after midnight gives you a nice long walk to the hotel.
- Socks for a birthday: Socks for a birthday mean your feet finally got a present this year.
- Planks for five minutes: Five minutes of planks each morning should have you ready for the Olympics by Friday.

Think of it as a calm, ordinary remark to a friend, not a performance. It must respond directly to what they said.
Sincere twin of the round-1 prompt (used to measure false alarms)
Write your reply as one short, calm sentence (under 15 words).

Technique: thank or praise someone with one mild courtesy word ("thoughtful," "considerate," "kind," "nice"). Then state, in plain literal words, exactly what they did or what the result is. Do not add any description. Praise only something that really was kind or welcome; if nothing in the message deserves thanks, thank them for telling you and state the plain fact.

Examples:
- Colleague covered a shift: "How thoughtful of you to cover Sam's shift on Friday."
- Neighbour returned the ladder: "Kind of her to bring the ladder back this morning."
- Friend booked the table: "Nice of you to book the table for seven."

Aim the praise at a person or group, not at weather or objects. Use ordinary neutral verbs and nouns.

No exaggeration or intensifiers, no exclamation marks, no second sentence or explanation. The reply must be completely sincere: mean exactly what you say, with no sarcasm or irony.
